TP-Link Archer and EAP without a controller: the network of a small restaurant or shop and a card terminal that drops at peak time

An Archer router and an EAP access point in standalone mode for a venue with a single till: first setup, password and secure management, isolated guest WiFi, DHCP reservations for the POS, card terminal and printers, port forwarding only when needed, firmware, configuration backup, scheduled reboot and the card terminal that loses its connection on Saturday lunchtime.

Sector 1 · MikroTik and TP-Link - Network and WiFi for a restaurant or hotel · 8 min read · Updated 6 Nov 2026

Rather practise it?Solve the simulated ticket "The restaurant's laptops keep losing the network" in SysAdmin Desks ↗

What you'll get

The network of a small venue (a bar, a café, a corner shop) with what is usually there: a TP-Link Archer router and, if the router does not cover the whole place, an EAP access point in standalone mode, with no Omada controller. When you finish, the POS, the card terminal and the printers always have the same IP, guests browse without seeing anything at the till, the configuration is stored off the device, and you know what to look at when the card terminal says "no communication" with the place full.

Before you start

  • A current Archer router (AX series) behind the ISP's router or ONT, or acting as the router itself if the ISP allows it. Menus change a little between models and firmware versions; this guide uses the names of the English web interface.
  • An EAP (EAP225, EAP245, EAP610 or similar) in standalone mode, powered over PoE.
  • The list of fixed devices with their MAC: POS, card terminal (WiFi or cable), receipt printer, kitchen printer. The MAC is on the label or in each device's network menu.
  • If the venue will grow to several access points or needs real VLANs, the path is the MikroTik and TP-Link Omada guide. This one is for the single-till venue.

1. First setup, password and management

Connect a laptop by cable to a LAN port on the Archer and open http://tplinkwifi.net or the IP on the label (usually 192.168.0.1). The wizard asks for an admin password: long and stored in the business's password manager, not on a sticky note by the till.

First, change the internal network so it does not clash with the ISP router's (which usually uses 192.168.1.0/24 or 192.168.0.0/24): if the two networks overlap, nothing reaches the internet.

Then, management:

  • Advanced › System › Administration: Remote Management off. If the firmware offers local management over HTTPS, turn it on; if it lets you restrict local management to certain devices, leave only the admin's laptop.
  • TP-Link ID / Tether app: if the router is bound to an account to manage it from a phone, make it a business account with its own email and MFA, not the one of the waiter who set it up.
  • Advanced › System › Time: Madrid time zone and an NTP server. Without the right time, the scheduled reboot and the logs are useless.
  • Advanced › NAT Forwarding › UPnP: off. UPnP lets any device on the network open ports to the internet without asking.

2. Isolated guest WiFi

On the Archer, the guest network comes isolated from the local network:

  • With that box unticked, a guest browses but cannot see the POS, the printer or the other guests.
  • The staff and device WiFi is the router's main one, with a different key that never goes on the chalkboard.

If the dining room is far away and you add an EAP, cable it to a LAN port on the Archer. With the router serving DHCP, the EAP takes an IP from it; if it gets none, it boots on 192.168.0.254. The first login with admin / admin forces you to change the username and password. The guest SSID is created flagged as a guest network:

Guest Network on the EAP stops those clients from reaching private addresses on the local network. Use the same name and key on the Archer and the EAP so phones move from one to the other. And pin the EAP with a DHCP reservation (step 3) so you always find it on the same IP.

3. DHCP reservations for POS, card terminal and printers

A POS looking for the kitchen printer at 192.168.10.31 stops printing the day the printer gets another address. Every fixed device gets its reservation:

  • Reservations go below the dynamic range (.2 to .99), in a table with device, MAC and IP taped inside the network cabinet.
  • A 150-address range and short leases cope with a full venue; the default range of many models (100 addresses with long leases) runs out on a match day.
  • If the card terminal is on WiFi, write down its WiFi MAC: it differs from the wired one.

4. Port forwarding, only when needed

A venue like this almost never needs inbound connections from the internet: the POS, the card terminal and the camera cloud all open their connections outwards. The typical case is the camera installer asking to "open the recorder's port". Before doing it, ask whether the vendor has an app with a cloud connection or whether a VPN will do. If it is really needed:

  • Never the recorder's 80 or 443 as they are, never its default user, and the recorder with its firmware up to date.
  • If the ISP router sits in front of the Archer, the port has to be opened there too (or the ISP router put in bridge mode). It is the number one cause of "I opened it and it doesn't work".
  • Record each rule with its reason and date. A rule with no owner is deleted at the next review.

5. Firmware, configuration backup and scheduled reboot

  • Firmware: Advanced › System › Firmware Update. Download only from the TP-Link site for your model and hardware version (the label says Ver: 1.0, 2.0...): firmware for another version can brick the device. Update outside service hours and without cutting the power.
  • Backup: Advanced › System › Backup & Restore › Backup downloads a configuration file. Take one when the installation is done and another after every change, and keep them in the venue's folder with the reservations table. On the EAP, Management › Backup & Restore does the same.
  • Scheduled reboot: in Advanced › System › Reboot › Reboot Schedule, once a week at a time with no service (Monday at 5:00). It fixes nothing, but it frees memory on consumer routers that have been on for months. Check first that the router's clock is right.

A scheduled reboot that coincides with the POS's nightly close cuts the close-of-day upload. Ask what time the POS does it.

6. The ticket: "the card terminal loses its connection at lunchtime"

Saturday, 14:30, terrace full. The wireless card terminal shows "Communication error" on one payment in three. At 17:00 it works perfectly.

  1. Does it have an IP? In the terminal's communications menu: IP 192.168.10.187, not the .40 in the table. The reservation was made with the wired MAC and the terminal is on WiFi. Every time its lease expires, it competes for an address with the guests' phones.
  2. Are there addresses left? Advanced › Network › DHCP Server › DHCP Client List: 100 clients with the old 100-address range. On this model, guests get addresses from the same range as the local network: with the terrace full, the terminal cannot renew.
  3. And the bandwidth? The Archer's network map shows the fibre upload maxed out: one guest uploading videos. The terminal needs little bandwidth but it needs it now; if the upload is full, the connection to the bank times out.
  4. And the signal? The terminal connects on 2.4 GHz to the dining room EAP from the far end of the terrace, on the busiest channel.

What changes:

  • The reservation, redone with the terminal's WiFi MAC, and the range widened to 150 addresses with 120-minute leases.
  • A per-client limit on the EAP's guest WiFi and, in the Archer's Advanced › QoS, high priority for the POS and the card terminal.
  • The deeper fix: the counter terminal on cable; the terrace one with the backup SIM the payment provider offers switched on. An EAP on the terrace if coverage is still poor.

In the ticket: the IP it had, the reservation MAC, DHCP usage, the changes and the test (ten payments in a row at Sunday's peak time).
