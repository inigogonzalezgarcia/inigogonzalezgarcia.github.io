Cisco WAP and Cisco Business Wireless: setup, SSID per VLAN, WPA2/WPA3 with RADIUS, guest portal, SNMP, firmware and guest clients with no IP

Which Cisco small-office models are still supported, setting up a CBW primary AP, each SSID in its VLAN over a trunk port, WPA2/WPA3 Personal versus Enterprise with RADIUS, the guest captive portal, SNMP and firmware, and guests who connect but get no IP.

Level 2 · Cisco WAP: small-office access points · 8 min read · Updated 6 Nov 2026

What you'll get

Setting up or inheriting the WiFi of a small office with Cisco access points from the small-business range: knowing whether the model in front of you is still supported, bringing up a Cisco Business Wireless primary AP, putting each SSID on its own VLAN, choosing between a shared key and RADIUS, giving visitors a network with a captive portal kept apart from the corporate one, monitoring them over SNMP and updating them. And solving the Monday ticket: guests connect to the WiFi but have no Internet because they get no IP.

Before you start

First, which model it is and where it is in its life:

FamilyModelsStatus (November 2026)
Old WAP 300 seriesWAP321, WAP371Retired years ago, no support or firmware
WAPWAP125, WAP150, WAP361, WAP571, WAP581End of sale in 2021; last date of support 31 August 2026
Cisco Business Wireless ACCBW140AC, CBW145AC, CBW240AC (and mesh extenders)End of sale 19 August 2026, no replacement announced; support until 31 August 2031
Cisco Business Wireless AXCBW150AX, CBW151AXM (extender)Wi-Fi 6 and WPA3; cannot share a network with CBW AC models
  • A WAP125 or WAP371 in production is a device without security patches: record it as a risk and plan its replacement. This guide uses the CBW interface, the one still supported; standalone WAPs have equivalent per-device menus (Wireless › Networks for SSIDs, Access Control › Guest Access for the portal).
  • In CBW, one AP acts as the Primary AP (the built-in controller) and manages the others. There can only be one CBW deployment per LAN, and CBW150AX units do not coexist with CBW140AC/240AC on the same network.
  • A DHCP server for the APs' management VLAN and for each SSID VLAN; RADIUS (NPS or FreeRADIUS) if you will use WPA2/WPA3-Enterprise.
  • The office VLANs: 10 management, 20 users, 40 guests.

1. Setting up the primary AP

When it boots from factory defaults (8-10 minutes until the LED blinks green), the AP broadcasts the CiscoBusiness-Setup network with the key cisco123:

  1. Connect to that network with a laptop and open http://ciscobusiness.cisco. Click Start.
  2. Create the administrator account (username and password cannot contain "cisco").
  3. Primary AP name, country, date, time and time zone. Mesh stays off unless you will use it.
  4. The first WiFi network and its key. It can be changed later.
  5. Apply: the AP reboots in 5-6 minutes. Then log in at https://ciscobusiness.cisco or https://<AP IP>.

The switch port for the primary AP is a trunk with the management VLAN untagged (native) and the SSID VLANs tagged. On a Catalyst:

  • The AP gets its management IP by DHCP on the native VLAN. Pin it with a reservation on the DHCP server: that is the address you will register in RADIUS and in monitoring.
  • Every AP gets the same port configuration. An AP on a different port broadcasts the SSID but cannot deliver that VLAN's traffic.
  • If the AP was first set up on an access port, when you turn the port into a trunk keep the VLAN it booted in as native, or you will lose contact with it.

2. Each SSID on its own VLAN

In the top-right corner, the Expert View icon shows all menus.

  • Use VLAN Tagging: Yes with VLAN 20 makes the AP tag that SSID's traffic as it leaves over the trunk. With No, the SSID uses the native VLAN, the management one: user devices would end up on the APs' network.
  • After each change, the save icon at the top right. Without it, the next reboot undoes your work.
  • Three or four SSIDs at most. Each one adds beacons in the air and takes capacity from all of them.

3. Security: Personal versus Enterprise

ModeHow the user gets inWhen
WPA2-PersonalOne shared key for everyoneDevices without a user (printers, POS); changing the key means touching them all
WPA3-PersonalShared key with SAE, resistant to dictionary attacksThe same, if every client supports it (CBW150AX)
WPA2/WPA3-EnterpriseEach person's user or certificate against RADIUSCorporate network: a leaver stops getting in when their account is disabled

For Enterprise, in the SSID's WLAN Security tab choose Enterprise security and point it at the RADIUS server (10.10.0.20, port 1812, and its shared secret). On the server:

  • The primary AP's management IP as a RADIUS client, with the same secret: it is the one sending the requests.
  • In NPS, a network policy for the GG-WiFi-Corp group with PEAP-MSCHAPv2 (domain username and password) or, better, EAP-TLS with a computer certificate. The server needs a certificate the laptops trust.
  • If the model and clients support it, WPA2/WPA3 mixed mode lets old and new laptops share the same SSID.

4. Guests with a captive portal

Visitors go on their own SSID, in their own VLAN, and only get out to the Internet.

  • Access Type: Web Consent (accept the terms), Email Address (leave an email), Local User Account (users created under Wireless Settings › WLAN Users), RADIUS or Social Login. For an office, Web Consent or temporary users handed out by reception.
  • The page text is changed under Wireless Settings › Guest WLANs (Page Headline and Page Message), with Preview to see it.
  • The real separation is done by the network, not the AP: VLAN 40 ends at the firewall with a rule that only allows Internet access and denies 10.0.0.0/8. Without that rule, the guest is inside the office.
  • On the DHCP server, the guest scope with short leases (2-4 hours): visitors come and go and a scope with 8-day leases runs out.

5. SNMP and firmware

  • SNMP: in Expert View, Advanced › SNMP. If v3 is offered, a user with authentication and encryption; if only v2c, a community of your own (never public) and access only from the monitoring IP. Monitoring watches the primary AP's IP and, through it, the state of the others.
  • Firmware: Management › Software Update. The primary AP downloads the image (from Cisco.com, from the PC over HTTP, or over TFTP/SFTP) and pushes it to every AP, which reboot: WiFi drops across the whole office for a few minutes. In a change window, with the release notes read and a configuration export taken beforehand.

6. The ticket: "guests connect to the WiFi but have no Internet"

Monday, 10:00. Reception reports that visitors connect to CONTOSO-INVITADOS, their phone says No Internet connection and the portal never appears. A test laptop shows a 169.254.x.x address: it got no IP.

Under Monitoring › Network Summary › Clients, the client shows as associated to the SSID but with no IP address. Associating with the AP works; what fails is behind VLAN 40. Walk the path in order:

  1. Does VLAN 40 reach every AP? Check which AP the client is on (same list) and that AP's switch port:

There it is: the floor 2 AP was added with a trunk that only allows 10 and 20. Guests on floor 1 get an IP; those on floor 2 do not. Add the VLAN without replacing the list:

  1. If the VLAN reaches every AP: check it exists on the trunk between floors and the core (show interfaces trunk) and that the firewall or core has its interface on VLAN 40 with DHCP (or the relay to the server).
  2. The scope: on the DHCP server, the guest scope full of active leases. With 250 addresses and 8-day leases, it runs out after a few weeks of visitors. Short leases and clearing expired ones.
  3. The SSID settings: Use VLAN Tagging: Yes and the right VLAN number. A 41 instead of 40 gives exactly the same symptom.

Check: the test laptop gets a 10.40.0.x address, the portal appears, and after accepting it browses but cannot reach any internal IP (try the file server's).

In the ticket: affected AP and port, the VLAN list before and after, the check from a client and, if it was the scope, the change to the lease duration.
