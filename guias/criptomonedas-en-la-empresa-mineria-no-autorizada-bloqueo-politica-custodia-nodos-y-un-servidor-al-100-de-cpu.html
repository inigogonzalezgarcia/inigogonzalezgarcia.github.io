Cryptocurrency in the company from IT's side: unauthorised mining, blocking, acceptable use, custody, nodes and a server at 100% CPU

How unauthorised mining is detected on endpoints, servers and cloud, how pools are blocked at DNS, proxy and firewall, what the acceptable-use policy says, which controls IT applies if the company holds crypto, how to run a node safely, and the incident steps for a server that hits 100% CPU every night.

Level 4 · Cryptocurrency in the company · 9 min read · Updated 6 Nov 2026

What you'll get

Treating cryptocurrency as what it is for IT: a security risk (the miner someone installs on your servers), a policy matter (what is allowed on company devices) and, if the business decides to hold it, an asset whose keys must be guarded like any other critical secret. With a miner incident from start to finish.

This guide is not financial or investment advice: it does not recommend buying, selling or mining any cryptocurrency.

Before you start

  • CPU monitoring for servers and endpoints (Zabbix, SolarWinds or whatever is in place) with at least a month of history.
  • An EDR or Microsoft Defender for Endpoint on every endpoint and server, and access to its console.
  • Control of internal DNS, the proxy or web filter and the perimeter firewall.
  • If there is cloud: read access to costs and to security alerts (Microsoft Defender for Cloud, Amazon GuardDuty).
  • For custody: Finance and Compliance in the conversation from the start. IT sets the controls; the decision to hold crypto-assets is not IT's.

1. What unauthorised mining looks like

There are two cases and they look little alike:

  • Cryptojacking: an attacker gets in through an exposed service (SSH with a weak password, Redis or the Docker API without authentication, an unpatched web app) and installs a miner, almost always for Monero with a variant of XMRig. They look for servers with plenty of CPU that nobody watches at night.
  • Internal use: someone on staff uses GPU machines, a lab server or the cloud account to mine for themselves.

The signals, from most to least reliable:

SignalWhere to look
CPU sustained at 90-100% with no business load, often at nightMonitoring: the 30-day graph changes shape on a specific day
Antivirus or EDR detectionDefender: CoinMiner families (Trojan:Win32/CoinMiner, Trojan:Linux/CoinMiner) or potentially unwanted applications
Outbound connections to pools over stratumFirewall and proxy: ports such as 3333, 4444, 5555 or 14444, or stratum+tcp:// in the process command line
DNS queries to pool domainsInternal DNS or DNS filter logs
Cloud bill rising on computeNew GPU instances, sometimes in a region the company does not use
Fans and temperatureThe user: "the laptop is hot and slow"

2. Blocking pools: DNS, proxy and firewall

Three layers, because a well-built miner tries several:

  • DNS: the DNS filter or security provider usually has a Cryptomining or Cryptocurrency mining category: set it to block. With Windows Server DNS, a query resolution policy drops queries to a list of domains you maintain:
  • Proxy or web filter: the same category in the browsing policies, which also stops in-browser mining scripts.
  • Firewall: what protects most is that servers do not have free outbound access to the Internet. An outbound rule per server or group with the destinations it needs (updates, business APIs) and a final logged deny. With that, the miner installs but cannot work, and the firewall log gives it away.
  • Endpoints: in Defender, potentially unwanted application (PUA) detection in block mode and network protection; the Defender and ASR rules guide explains how to turn them on without breaking anything.

A server that can only talk to what it needs is the best defence against miners, and against almost everything else too.

3. The acceptable-use policy

A few lines in the systems acceptable-use policy, reviewed by HR and Legal, make clear what is allowed and what the consequences are:

  • Mining cryptocurrency with any company resource is forbidden: endpoints, servers, cloud accounts, office electricity and the network, including browser extensions and apps that "share resources" in exchange for rewards.
  • Installing wallets, nodes or trading software on corporate devices is forbidden unless explicitly authorised for a business function.
  • Using company email, credentials or devices for personal accounts on exchanges is forbidden.
  • IT may monitor CPU use, processes and traffic to detect these activities, within what the privacy policy and employment law allow.
  • Suspicions are reported to IT or Security through the usual channel.

The approved software list and blocking in the device manager (Intune, MECM) are what enforce the policy; the text only explains it.

4. If the company holds crypto-assets: custody and IT controls

The decision belongs to management and Finance; IT's job is making sure the keys are neither lost nor used by the wrong people. The usual options:

OptionWho holds the keysMain riskIT control
Exchange or custodianThe providerThe provider fails or is attackedAccounts with hardware-key MFA, withdrawals only to allow-listed addresses, two people to approve
Hardware walletThe company, on a deviceLosing the device and the seed phrasePhysical custody, seed phrase outside every system
Multisig (for example 2 of 3)Several people with separate devicesCoordinating signers and recoverySigners at different sites, written and tested procedure

In the EU, crypto-asset service providers need authorisation under the MiCA regulation: checking the custodian has it is a question for Compliance.

The non-negotiable controls for the seed phrase (12 or 24 words that regenerate every key):

  • Never in digital form: not in a shared password manager, not in a photo, not in an email, not in a cloud document.
  • Stamped in metal or written on paper, in a safe, with a log of who accesses it. If it is split between people, none of them can rebuild it alone.
  • Dual control: no single person can move funds. With multisig the scheme guarantees it; with a hardware wallet, the procedure and access to the safe do.
  • Before every large transfer, a small test transaction and the destination address checked on the device screen, not on the clipboard (there is malware that swaps it).
  • A recovery test once a year with a new device, recorded like any backup test.
  • Leavers: when a signer leaves the company, their key is rotated the way a privileged account is.

5. Running a node: lab and production

Sometimes a development team needs its own node (for example Bitcoin Core) to validate transactions or test an integration. The rules change depending on where:

  • Lab: on an isolated VLAN, on the test network (testnet=1 or signet=1) rather than mainnet, with no production data and a retirement date.
  • Production: a dedicated server, no wallet on the node (disablewallet=1), the RPC interface on localhost only and authenticated, patched like any other server and watched by monitoring.
  • Port 8333 (P2P) may be opened inbound if the node must accept connections; 8332 (RPC) never. An exposed RPC is a direct way in.
  • The full chain takes hundreds of gigabytes and grows every day; prune mode keeps only recent blocks if the application does not need history.
  • rpcauth stores a hash of the password, not the password; the project's own rpcauth.py script generates it.

6. The ticket: "lnx-app07 hits 100% CPU every night"

Monday, 8:15. Monitoring has alerted three nights running: lnx-app07, an Ubuntu application server, climbs to 100% CPU around 23:00 and drops at 7:00. Nobody has changed anything in the deployment.

  1. Look without touching. Before killing anything, record what is happening:

A process with a system-sounding name in /tmp, deleted from disk after starting, running as redis, connected to port 3333 and with a cron job that reinstalls it every half hour. It is a miner.

  1. Open a security incident, not just a performance ticket: tell Security and the application owner. From here on, every step goes in the incident log with its time.
  1. Contain. Isolate the server in the EDR (Isolate device) or, without one, a firewall rule cutting all outbound traffic except the management console. The miner stops working and downloading, and no evidence is lost.
  1. Keep evidence before cleaning: a copy of the binary from /proc/48211/exe, its hash (sha256sum), the crontab, the process and connection lists, /var/log/auth.log and the Redis configuration. If the server is a VM, a snapshot.
  1. Find the way in. The user tells you: Redis. redis-cli CONFIG GET bind returns 0.0.0.0 and requirepass is empty; a rule on the site firewall opened 6379 to the Internet for a test months ago. Also look for persistence in ~/.ssh/authorized_keys, /etc/cron.d/, systemctl list-timers and new services in /etc/systemd/system/.
  1. Eradicate and recover. The safe option is to rebuild the server from a clean image and restore the application data, not clean it by hand: you do not know what else the attacker left. Redis comes back listening on 127.0.0.1 only, with a password and without the firewall rule. Credentials that were on the server are changed.
  1. Check the scope: the section 1 search across the whole estate and the DNS logs for the same domain or the IP 203.0.113.45. If the server held personal data, deciding whether to notify follows the ISO 27001, NIS2 and GDPR guide.

In the ticket: symptom, process and connection, way in (Redis exposed without a password), containment, evidence kept, rebuild, firewall changes, scope checked and the task to review every "temporary" inbound rule.
